One idea, one source each.

01

What every civilisation knew

Many traditions marked a second birth: the sacred thread of the twice-born, worn next to the skin, is one. Anthropologists call these rites of passage. Victor Turner noticed that modern life swapped them for “liminoid” experiences we choose alone, and rarely mark.

Only you can name yours.

Source: Dvija; Victor Turner

02

Why it begins with an ending

William Bridges observed that every transition starts not with a beginning but with letting go. The new can't begin until something of the old has been released.

Only you can name yours.

Source: William Bridges, What Is Transition?

03

The in-between

Arnold van Gennep described a rite of passage in three parts: separation, transition, return. Turner called the middle “liminal”: no longer, not yet. It's where the change actually happens.

Only you can name yours.

Source: Arnold van Gennep; Victor Turner

04

Why you can't see it until after

The philosopher L. A. Paul writes of transformative experiences: changes you can't understand in advance, only from the other side. The Greek metanoia, a change of mind, first carried the sense of afterthought.

Only you can name yours.

Source: L. A. Paul; Etymonline

05

The silent transformations

William Miller and Janet C'de Baca studied people whose lives turned and stayed turned. Many of those who changed most had told almost no one.

Only you can name yours.

Source: Miller & C'de Baca

06

Two traditions, one word

In Sanskrit, pratyabhijñā; in Latin, re-cognoscere. Both mean to know again. Kashmir Shaiva philosophy holds that the deepest realisation is recognising what was already true.

Only you can name yours.

Source: Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy, Kashmiri Shaivism

07

Why generative art

Sāṃkhya holds that the oil is already in the seed. A generative piece works the same way: a seed unfolds, through a process, into a form no one could see in advance.

Only you can name yours.

Source: Sāṃkhya

08

Shared without being exposed

Abhinavagupta wrote of sādhāraṇīkaraṇa: art lets a feeling be shared without belonging to one person's private history. The form can be seen while the story stays yours.

Only you can name yours.

Source: Abhinavagupta on rasa
